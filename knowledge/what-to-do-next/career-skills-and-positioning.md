# Skills growth, positioning, job search and interviews

Mostly career content for a human. Included are the learning/tooling rules (relevant to an agent choosing tools and depth), the six "key findings" framework and the job-board, red-flag and interview material. Page numbers are PDF pages.

## 1. Core ideas
1. Skill is the first differentiator; pick one "hill" (skill path) and climb it before adding adjacent skills; strong basics first (p.9-10).
2. Do not become a jack of all trades; master one topic, then move on; keep learning; allocate daily time for learning (p.10-11).
3. Do not switch tools too early: new tools bring a learning curve; learn it to a good extent, then switch (example: Photoshop -> Sketch, p.11).
4. Combine only related skills ("tree": trunk is the major skill, leaves/fruit are relevant additions); don't mix unrelated ones (3ds Max + HTML example) (p.11-12).
5. Six recurring findings for freelance UI work: Define, Experience, Visibility, Money, Time, Networking (p.43-54).
6. UX/UI work is more than interfaces: problem-solving, empathy, business approaches, data analysis (p.103).
7. Job-posting red flags depend on personal tolerance (p.102-105).
8. UX/UI hires come from many backgrounds; non-design backgrounds that train empathy (nurse, customer service, social worker, educator, psychology) are valuable (p.110-112).

## 2. Rules

- **RULE** Choose one skill path ("hill") first; learn the latest tools, techniques, methods for it; then expand into related skills.
  **Why** Depth differentiates; chasing whatever earns money this month (3ds Max, Android) scatters effort.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a (planning). **Source** (p.9-10) [engine note: for an agent, prefer depth in the user's stack over adding tools]

- **RULE** Make the basics strong before advanced techniques.
  **Why** Newbies "don't get much grip on the basics".
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.10)

- **RULE** Do not switch tools/technologies as soon as they appear; learn it well, account for the slow early learning curve, then switch.
  **Why** Early switching is slow and shallow.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.10-11) (opinion)

- **RULE** Add only skills related to your major skill (e.g., HTML/CSS with UI design), not unrelated ones.
  **Why** Related skills compound ("tree" metaphor).
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.11-12) (opinion)

- **RULE** Allocate a daily block of time for learning; never stop learning.
  **Why** Technology and tooling move fast.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.10-11)

- **RULE** Be able to explain, on request, the process, rationale and daily workflow behind a project; developers must be able to explain method/code choices.
  **Why** Interviewers (and clients) always ask.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.14-15)

- **RULE** Use a wide range of design stakeholders: collaborate with management, test users, technical department; be able to explain design concepts (user persona, testing, layout) to marketers, engineers, business analysts.
  **Why** UX/UI is collaborative even for a department of one.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.117-118)

- **RULE** Be honest about methodology experience (Lean, Agile, Design Thinking). A critical view is fine if backed by logical arguments.
  **Why** The recruiter is testing workflow fit and reasoning.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.116-117)

- **RULE** In job-search answers, explain why a project was best or worst; difficulty that forced a new approach is a good story.
  **Why** Shows motivation and problem-solving.
  **Applies to** n/a (interview). **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.118-119)

- **RULE** Evaluate job postings against your own tolerance: lists of every imaginable duty; weird or impossible requirements; absurd tasks; lack of company diversity; unrealistic wages; who the UX team reports to.
  **Why** Employers often don't understand UX/UI; flags show mismatch.
  **Applies to** n/a (job search). **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.102-105; diagram p.103)

- **RULE** Check job boards regularly (the book suggests once or twice a week for IxDA). Boards named: Dribbble (full-time + contract; freelance listings PRO-only), Behance (international, no extra fee for freelance), IxDA (~300 ads at time of writing, mostly US), RemoteOK (remote), Ideate Labs (0-2 years' experience; small hard-to-read font), Design Jobs Board (long-standing; vivid colours), Remotive (~2000 listings; can hide US-only), AngelList (startups), Interns.Design (internships).
  **Why** Different boards serve different regions/seniority.
  **Applies to** n/a. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.91-101)

- **RULE** Stay visible: attend local meet-ups and conferences, write about design experiences, be active on social platforms (Twitter, LinkedIn, Instagram, Medium, Facebook, Slack, Dribbble).
  **Why** Visibility builds personal brand and online communication skill.
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.45-47)

- **RULE** Network and keep in touch with clients, peers and coworkers; they refer and are sources for rates of comparable jobs.
  **Why** Referrals; "best method to get good work is to produce good work and have other people tell others".
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.52-54)

- **RULE** Final checklist for a successful UI freelancer: be honest; know your UX/UI process; love helping people; deliver on promises; take pride in work; don't sell yourself short; weed out bad clients; define the scope first.
  **Why** Book's own summary.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.54-56) (opinion)

### Audit additions: page images (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** When asked to explain your design process, cover the three points in the book's p.115 diagram: (1) make it clear you know what you are talking about (a broad overview of the UX/UI process), (2) share your work process and how you address issues, (3) share your thought process; and show you are not intimidated by challenges and deal with them calmly.
  **Why** The recruiter uses this question to verify theory and practice; "they genuinely want to know about your thought process" (p.115-116, printed 113-114).
  **Applies to** all (an agent presenting a design: overview, steps taken, problems met and how they were handled, reasoning). **Check** delivery notes contain process, issues handled and rationale; visual/manual. **Source** (p.115 diagram, p.116) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Before going independent, answer the three Experience questions in writing: "Do I have the necessary experience to venture out alone?", "Do I have a good name yet?", "Will my work be valued?"; then keep expanding services, because experience "does not happen overnight".
  **Why** The book frames these as the questions that block most new freelancers; the Cowper and McDerment quote says you grow by pushing yourself to find new ways to help clients (p.44-45).
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.44-45) (audit 2026-10-15)

## 4. Dos and Don'ts

- Do: say how you worked and what happened. Don't: give a tool-list-only answer (p.107, 111).
- Do: learn one hill. Don't: mix 3ds Max with HTML (p.11).
- Do: 'No' to unethical clients. Don't: take clients who ask for copyright violation (p.89-90).

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls

- Advice to delay adopting new tools (p.10-11) conflicts with the "learn the latest tools" line on p.9-10; book reconciles: learn the latest tools *for your chosen path*, but switch only after learning the new tool well. [engine note: apply to dependency choices; adopt new framework only when stable and understood.]
- p.16-17: learn English by watching films with subtitles; "don't look into the grammar while speaking". Personal teaching opinion, not applicable to the engine.
- Red-flag weighting is personal (p.103-105).
