# Freelance business, pricing, proposals and starting an agency

Business content with limited bearing on how an agent designs. Captured for completeness and for the parts that shape scope, estimation and client handoff. Items that only matter to a human freelancer are marked "human-only". Page numbers are PDF pages.

## 1. Core ideas
1. Freelancing = self-employed, several clients at once, you manage priorities, time, workload and taxes (p.6-7).
2. Pricing models listed: hourly rate, day rate, fixed project price, hourly rate + ballpark figure, buckets of hours (p.48). No model is declared best.
3. Sell the solution and its impact on the client's business, not hours; position it as an investment, not an expense (p.50, 52). Opposing view: many swear by hourly billing (p.48).
4. Never work without getting paid; "Don't work for nothing. Ever." (p.47, 56). Stage invoicing protects against non-payment (p.83).
5. Do not run on endless long hours: it hurts mental health, social life and quality, and quality predicts future work volume (p.51).
6. Proposal = what, when, by whom, how much, what's excluded, schedule, terms (p.85-86).
7. Agency = several people; freelancing is one. Decide offering, niche, location, name, structure, money (p.58-90).
8. Niche positioning gives authority, shorter learning curve, differentiation (p.65-66).
9. Reputation compounding: good work + referrals is "the truly best method to get good work" (Reichelt, p.53).

## 2. Rules

- **RULE** Put a written proposal in place before starting work: delivery details (what, when, by whom, number of meetings), the client's role in keeping the schedule, deliverables, cost, payment dates, what is excluded/extra, a realistic schedule, and T&Cs covering permissions/releases, copyright and ownership.
  **Why** The proposal sets expectations; T&Cs are the fallback for late payment or mid-project cancellation.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** proposal contains each listed item. **Source** (p.85-86)

- **RULE** Explain the project stages and ways of working in plain language; many clients have never commissioned design.
  **Why** A client who understands the process cooperates better.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** manual. **Source** (p.85)

- **RULE** Invoice on completion of each stage, not only at the end.
  **Why** Protects you if a client doesn't pay; aids cash flow and forecasting.
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.83)

- **RULE** Base bids on expected hours (from timesheets) so you have room to go back to the client when scope creeps.
  **Why** Hours-based bids tie price to scope.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** quote lists hour estimate per stage. **Source** (p.82-83)

- **RULE** Compute a minimum hourly rate = annual survival budget (living costs + business overheads) / billable hours. Example: 8 h/day, 5 d/week, 4 weeks off = 1,920 h/yr; ~40% for operations (finding clients, books, planning) leaves 1,152 billable h.
  **Why** Time is what a service business sells; undercharging loses money.
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.80-81) (see open question: the book's result does not match its numbers)

- **RULE** Test a rate against client reaction: if more than 1 in 4 people wince, slow down (ease off the rate); if fewer than 1 in 4 wince, raise it.
  **Why** Wince rate is a market signal (Reichelt quote).
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.49)

- **RULE** When a client wants a quote on the spot, it is fine to gather needs, take notes and return the next day with a proposal.
  **Why** Better to examine requirements before committing to a budget.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.50)

- **RULE** Do not undervalue yourself because a job took less time; value = skill and impact, not minutes.
  **Why** Quoted verbatim in spirit: "you are valuable as a person with skills and qualities" (p.45-46).
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.45-46, 52) (opinion)

- **RULE** Track hours on long engagements and keep detailed notes; they inform future estimates and mistakes.
  **Why** Learning loop for estimation.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** time log. **Source** (p.51)

- **RULE** Make exceptions on rate only for a portfolio-growing project, a client you really want, or a cause you believe in; be wary of clients who bargain you down in exchange for promised future work.
  **Why** Design is competitive; many clients exploit this.
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.83-84) (opinion)

- **RULE** Weed out bad clients and "don't accept every project" (see also `brief-scope-and-delivery-process.md`).
  **Why** Protects brand and sanity.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.56, 89-90)

- **RULE** Pick a niche when founding an agency: a specific vertical gives authority, a shorter learning curve, and a differentiator; verify enough demand exists and will grow.
  **Why** Generalists have no value proposition beyond "we do good work" (Racki quote, p.65).
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.65-67) (opinion, cited authority)

- **RULE** If you offer one service, form referral arrangements with non-competing agencies offering complementary services.
  **Why** Partners send you work they can't do.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.64)

- **RULE** Choose an agency name with checks for: existing trademarks; incorporation availability in your state; domain and social handles; long-term fit (not restrictive). Law differs by country; seek a business attorney.
  **Why** Name drives all branding; conflicts are costly.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** domain and handle availability. **Source** (p.73-75)

- **RULE** List what you do as a freelancer and identify what you cannot do when scaled; hire for each gap: workload -> assistant; finding customers -> salesperson/account rep; technical -> technical co-founder; managing staff -> partner/CEO; running the business -> accountant, attorney, VA.
  **Why** Defines hiring in order of need.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.76)

- **RULE** Pick partners with complementary skills or the same skills according to your gaps; split workload evenly; collaborate on design decisions even if one partner owns suppliers/dev.
  **Why** Complementary abilities widen services; uneven load breaks partnerships.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.77-78)

- **RULE** Choose suppliers who are specialists (not salespeople), share your vision and ethics, and nurture them as an extension of your team.
  **Why** Output quality depends on suppliers; good ones handle problems professionally.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.78)

- **RULE** Remote staff: state in advance whether in-office work will be required; use time-tracking tooling rather than frequent check-ins (book describes Hubstaff: time tracking, optional random screenshots, activity detection, payroll, invoicing, tasks with weekly sprints/checklists/daily standups; free up to 5 team members).
  **Why** Check-ins are inefficient; surprises on office policy upset staff.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.69-72) (the Hubstaff text reads as promotional)

- **RULE** Seek advice from an accountant/lawyer on taxes, licences, overheads, invoicing before going full-time freelance; keep company expenses deductible.
  **Why** Legal/tax exposure.
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.50, 56, 81)

### Audit additions: page images (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Start an agency offering the service you already supplied as a freelancer, then expand the offer as the business and team grow (a writer starts a content agency, a designer a web design or branding firm).
  **Why** Transitioning into an agency built on talents you already have is "a simple but effective strategy" for deciding what to offer (p.63).
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.63) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Build a referral network with partner agencies before hiring in-house for adjacent services; pass work you could do in-house to someone who does it better.
  **Why** Catapult Creative's early misstep, per creative director Daniel Rizer, was "employing in-house instead of creating a professional network"; partners bring new work, which employees have little motivation to do (p.64). Extends the existing referral rule.
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.64) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Decide the headquarters early (US company law is mostly state-level; a remote agency is governed by its home state), check names in the USPTO Trademark Electronic Search System and the state filing office, and outside the US study local law or ask a business attorney.
  **Why** Location sets the governing law; trademark violation brings "significant legal fees and penalties"; the book notes its trademark and incorporation advice is US-only (p.69, p.74-75).
  **Applies to** agency (human-only). **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.69, p.74-75) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Keep a new agency simple at first: you will not run at full capacity right away, so save money (the authors worked from a flat and met clients at the clients' offices before renting a studio).
  **Why** "Make your Goals Attractive" (p.81).
  **Applies to** agency. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.81) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** If talking about money makes you uncomfortable, ask other freelancers how they handle it and practise speaking (the book points to TED.com).
  **Why** Money talk is "where several freelancers have the most difficulty" and is critical when something goes wrong mid-project (p.47, p.50, p.56).
  **Applies to** human-only. **Check** n/a. **Source** (p.47, p.50, p.56) (audit 2026-10-15)

## 4. Dos and Don'ts

- Do: staged invoicing; Don't: invoice everything at the end (p.83).
- Do: offer lower rate for portfolio-expanding project; Don't: accept a discount for vague future work (p.83-84).
- Do: specialize in a vertical (hotels -> travel and tourism expertise, p.66); Don't: pitch "we do good work" to everyone (p.65).
- Platforms listed as freelance sources: Kimp, Fiverr, Upwork, Toptal, ManyPixels, 99designs, Onlinejobs.ph, Freelancer.com, DesignCrowd, Design Pickle (p.30-41). Characteristics: Fiverr cheap/quick; Upwork large with search filters, work diary, forum, mobile app; Toptal strict screening; 99designs contest-based over 90 design types; Freelancer.com contests and skill tests; Design Pickle one designer, overnight revisions; ManyPixels strong for online/mobile app design.

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls

- Hourly vs value-based pricing: the book gives both sides and chooses neither (p.48).
- Real office vs remote agency: Racki says a real office aids credibility with big-fee clients; remote is increasingly common (p.69-70).
- Using your own name as agency name is one founder's opinion, not a rule (p.73).
- Some sections (p.30-41 platform descriptions, p.58-61 definitions) are generic web-copy style and may be dated.
- Nothing here affects visual output; the engine should take only the scope/proposal/estimation ideas.
