# Brief, scope and delivery process

The book has no visual-design rules. Its process content is freelance-client advice. Rules here are the ones that transfer to an agent that takes a one-line brief and ships front-end work. Where the mapping from "freelancer + client" to "agent + founder" is my own, it is tagged [engine note]. Page numbers are PDF pages (printed page = PDF page - 1).

## 1. Core ideas
1. Understanding the brief is a precondition for any result: if you cannot read what the brief says, "you are not going to do well in your end result" (p.14).
2. Interrogate before you build: talk to the client in depth before any contract or quote, and "never be hesitant to bombard someone with questions" (p.87). Ask "Why?" repeatedly to reach the client's problems, ambitions, motivations (p.52).
3. Restate the brief in your own words. Few clients supply a formal brief; even when they do, rewriting it surfaces misunderstandings and missing points before creative work starts (p.87-88).
4. Define scope in writing first. The closing checklist says "Define the scope of the project first" and "Write down the scope of your projects initially" (p.56). Scope also gives you grounds to push back on creep (p.82-83).
5. Define your own process, success criteria and differentiator before starting (p.44 "Define" list).
6. Break large work into small pieces and finish one or two per day; this is how deadlines are met (p.24-25).
7. Under-promise on time: add 2-3 days of buffer to a 7-day estimate, quote 10, deliver on 7 (p.24).
8. Involve the client in decisions. A client inside the process accepts fresh ideas more readily; behave as partner, not supplier (p.88). The client knows their customers better than you do (p.88).
9. Deliver in stages, each with a defined completion point (design concept, development, implementation) (p.83).
10. Do not take work you cannot finish. Tell the client as soon as you know you cannot complete or hit a deadline (p.21-23, 27-28).

## 2. Rules

- **RULE** Before producing anything, restate the brief in your own words (goals, objectives, audience, deliverables) and surface it for confirmation or correction.
  **Why** Misunderstandings and missing points are far cheaper to catch before creative work than after.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** an explicit "brief restated" artifact exists before the first design/code step; it lists goals and objectives. **Source** (p.87-88) [mapping to agent = engine note]

- **RULE** Ask clarifying questions up front until you can say exactly what the client wants; do not start on an ambiguous brief.
  **Why** "Understanding what they want is critical; else, you won't be able to offer it" (p.87).
  **Applies to** all. **Check** list of open questions is empty or each has a stated assumption before build. **Source** (p.87) [engine note: when the founder cannot be asked, write each assumption down explicitly]

- **RULE** Ask "Why?" repeatedly about each requirement to find the underlying problem, ambition and motivation. Examine requirements before committing to a solution or budget, because the client may not understand why they need the website/app.
  **Why** The stated request is often a proxy for a business problem.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** each major requirement has a recorded "why". **Source** (p.50, 52)

- **RULE** Write the scope down first: what is delivered, what is NOT included, and what costs extra.
  **Why** An explicit boundary lets you go back to the client when scope creeps or they ask for things "that weren't in your original proposal" (p.82-83, 85).
  **Applies to** all. **Check** a scope doc with an explicit exclusions list exists. **Source** (p.56, 82-83, 85)

- **RULE** State deliverables concretely: what will be produced, by whom, by when, how many meetings, and what the client must do to keep the schedule on track.
  **Why** Many clients have never commissioned design; the process must be explained to them.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** deliverables list with owner and date per item. **Source** (p.85)

- **RULE** Include a realistic schedule that accounts for other work running in parallel, to avoid bottlenecks. "Above all, avoid over-promising."
  **Why** Missed deadlines damage trust more than a longer honest estimate.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** schedule exists; no item lacks an estimate. **Source** (p.86)

- **RULE** When estimating time, add a buffer: example 7 days of work -> quote 10 days (add 2-3 days). Deliver early against the quoted date.
  **Why** Early delivery against a padded promise reads as speed and wins ratings; (the book cites Walmart/Amazon delivery padding as the same trick).
  **Applies to** all. **Check** quoted duration = estimate + 2 to 3 days on a 7-day job (~30-40% pad). **Source** (p.24) (opinion/personal tip)

- **RULE** Split large projects into small pieces and complete one or two pieces per day; do not postpone starting a big job.
  **Why** Procrastination on large jobs is the failure mode; small pieces make progress measurable.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** task list granularity: each task completable in about a day. **Source** (p.24-25)

- **RULE** Cut a project into essential stages (design concept, development, implementation, etc.) and close each stage explicitly (invoice/sign-off on completion).
  **Why** Each stage gets a clean sign-off and protects against late or non-payment; also helps cash-flow forecasting (p.83).
  **Applies to** all. **Check** stage list with completion criterion per stage. **Source** (p.83) [engine note: for an agent, stage sign-off = review gate before next stage]

- **RULE** Involve the client in design decisions and learn their business and customers before proposing solutions.
  **Why** An involved client is receptive to new ideas; they know their own customers better than you do.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** decision points listed with who approves. **Source** (p.88)

- **RULE** Do not accept projects you do not know how to finish; confirm you can reach the finish line first. Prefer genres you know, master them, then move to the next topic.
  **Why** Taking work to "get the job first" leads to abandoned projects and bad ratings (the p.22-23 story: a freelancer claimed grid/Bootstrap skill, had none, ghosted for three days then quit).
  **Applies to** all. **Check** capability self-assessment before accept. **Source** (p.22-23, 27-28)

- **RULE** If you cannot finish or will miss a deadline, say so immediately; do not waste the client's time.
  **Why** Early notice lets the client re-plan.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** blockers raised at first occurrence, not at deadline. **Source** (p.21-22)

- **RULE** Answer client messages promptly and professionally: balanced tone ("not too strict or too funny"). On disagreement stay patient and composed; prefer a call/face-to-face over text.
  **Why** Calls de-escalate; text hardens conflicts.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a mechanically. visual/manual. **Source** (p.20-22)

- **RULE** Use plain, complete sentences and full words in client communication; avoid slang/abbreviations ("u", "DIY") because clients from other countries may not understand them.
  **Why** Non-native readers misinterpret shorthand.
  **Applies to** all (reports, commit messages, handoff notes). **Check** grep for "ur", "u ", "DIY" style shorthand in outbound text. **Source** (p.21)

- **RULE** Keep communication short and specific: enough information for the other person to understand fully, no excess or useless information; double-check before sending (spell/grammar checkers are "not foolproof").
  **Why** Brevity plus precision prevents misreads.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** manual re-read of each outbound message. **Source** (p.17-18)

- **RULE** Keep timesheets / track time per task; base future estimates on similar past projects; use them to explain cost and to detect a project running over budget.
  **Why** Past data makes quotes faster and more accurate; shows which work types are profitable.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** time log exists and estimates reference it. **Source** (p.51, 82)

- **RULE** Before the first working session write answers to: your process; why you take the work; which projects you will/won't take (and that "no" is allowed); your role in the client meeting; what success means to you AND the customer; what makes you stand out.
  **Why** Undefined process/success criteria cause drift; this is the book's #1 recurring theme ("Define").
  **Applies to** all. **Check** written answers to the 6 items exist. **Source** (p.43-44)

- **RULE** Learn when to say NO: no time/capacity, unrealistic expectations of budget/timescale/results (do not start until aligned), unethical activity or request to violate copyright, no chemistry.
  **Why** Protects brand, client relationship and sanity.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** explicit accept/decline decision with reason. **Source** (p.89-90)

- **RULE** When capacity is full, ask the client whether they are willing to wait before declining or overextending; if there is no urgent deadline, book the work ahead.
  **Why** Taking on more while swamped "will only disappoint your clients"; a client without an urgent deadline who wants to work with you lets you schedule ahead (p.89).

## 4. Dos and Don'ts

- Do: rewrite the brief in your own words and send it back. Don't: begin creative work on an unconfirmed brief (p.87-88).
- Do: say "I can't deliver this" early. Don't: go silent. Story p.22-23: freelancer misstated CSS/grid skills, took the contract, went quiet 3 days, then terminated without explanation.
- Do: quote buffer, deliver early. Don't: over-promise (p.24, 86).
- Do: pick projects you can finish. Don't: take unfamiliar work "to get a first project" (p.27-28).

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls

- Buffer advice (p.24) is the author's personal tip ("if you're as lazy as I am"); padding by 30-40% is one habit, not a standard. [engine note: for an agent, uncertainty ranges beat padding; state the estimate and its confidence].
- Pricing/billing detail is skipped here except the estimation link; see `freelance-business-and-agency.md`.
- p.28-29 suggests choosing projects you love over difficult portfolio-building ones when tired, and that "getting paid less for the job you love is more than ..." This is opinion/motivation, not a rule for the engine.
- The rules say "ask many questions"; an autonomous agent that cannot ask should record assumptions instead. [engine note]
- The brief-restatement rule has nothing to say about visual direction; the engine's pipeline (brief -> direction) must take its direction rules from the design books.
