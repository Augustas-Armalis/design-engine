# Presenting work, resumes, portfolios and case studies

Only three parts of the book touch design craft: mock-up presentation (p.19-20), resume design (p.105-111) and portfolio/case-study structure (p.112-113). The resume advice is effectively a mini design brief for a reading-first, content-first document and transfers to portfolio/case-study pages an agent might build. Page numbers are PDF pages.

## 1. Core ideas
1. Presentation changes perceived quality: even a "very simple logo" has a bigger impact if presented well (p.19).
2. Show design in real context (mock-ups) so the viewer can imagine it in use; present as a complete package, e.g. the final design on a phone for an app (p.19-20).
3. Restraint beats creativity in a document read by tired reviewers: avoid bright colours, striking shapes, unconventional layouts; "content should take centre stage" (p.106).
4. Show process, not just output. A visual-only portfolio cannot show problem-solving, empathy, business reasoning and data analysis (p.103, 112); portfolio + case studies together show visual skill and thinking.
5. Outcomes over tasks: state achievements and what you learned, not duties (p.107-108, 111-112).
6. Tailor emphasis per audience without lying (p.108-110).
7. Explaining design decisions to non-designers (marketers, engineers, analysts) is part of the job (p.117-118; p.14-15 interview "explain your process / idea behind this project").
8. Brevity and clarity in written communication; check body language and listening for spoken communication (p.15-18).

## 2. Rules

- **RULE** Present each design inside a realistic context mock-up (device frame for an app/site, table scene for a card) rather than as a bare artboard; present the final result as one complete package.
  **Why** "Mock-ups allow clients to see your design in context and in real world", which affects their imagination and thinking.
  **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI | mobile (deliverable previews). **Check** deliverable preview shows the design inside a device/frame or real surface; visual. **Source** (p.19-20)

- **RULE** Prefer high-quality (premium) mock-ups over weak free ones when the impression matters; the book names ThemeForest-type marketplaces.
  **Why** Quality of presentation lifts perceived quality of the work.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.20) (opinion)

- **RULE** For a reading-first document (resume, CV, case-study page), do not overdesign: no bright colours, no striking shapes, no unconventional layouts; use a "sober & direct" version; let content lead.
  **Why** Reviewers are "most likely tired and uninterested in a remarkable visual experience"; anything that diverts attention from the text is frowned on.
  **Applies to** reading-first pages (resume, docs, long-form). **Check** palette is neutral (text on plain background); no layout deviating from a single-column or simple two-column grid; visual. **Source** (p.105-106) [numeric thresholds are engine notes; book gives none]

- **RULE** Prioritize readability and a clear visual hierarchy; no long paragraphs, no confusing layouts; the viewer must grasp immediately what they are looking at.
  **Why** Scanning reviewers decide fast.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** headings differ visibly in size/weight from body; paragraph length short (book gives no number). **Source** (p.106-108)

- **RULE** Write each project entry to show how: "Instead of 'I designed the navigation system', show how you did it: how did you pull it off, what did it take, why was it important."
  **Why** Process evidence beats task statements.
  **Applies to** all copy (case studies, project cards). **Check** entry contains method + reason, not only a verb + noun. **Source** (p.107)

- **RULE** Describe achievements and learnings, not tasks: what you completed, how long it took, what you learned and why it matters.
  **Why** Recruiters want evidence, not job descriptions.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** each project entry has an outcome statement. **Source** (p.111-112)

- **RULE** Let writing reflect the author's voice (laid-back/funny or serious/direct) while remaining objective and concise; avoid grammar/spelling errors.
  **Why** Personality survives even in efficient layouts.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** spell/grammar pass. **Source** (p.106-108) (opinion)

- **RULE** Tailor emphasis to the target role/audience (e.g., data-driven research for UX, Agile for process-driven teams) but do not lie.
  **Why** One golden opportunity justifies re-framing.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** n/a (judgement). **Source** (p.108-110)

- **RULE** Pair a portfolio (visual skill) with case studies (thinking). Make case studies easy to read; start with the strongest project; keep it basic and to the point.
  **Why** Visual-only showcases (Dribbble/Behance style) omit problem-solving, empathy, business approach, data analysis.
  **Applies to** landing page (portfolio/case-study pages). **Check** first project in list is the strongest; each case study exists, not just images. **Source** (p.102-103, 112)

- **RULE** A case study answers five questions: what made the project difficult; how you overcame it; your logic and thought process; what you learned and how you apply it now; what effect the work had.
  **Why** This is the structure the book gives for case-study content.
  **Applies to** landing page (case-study template). **Check** page contains five labelled sections or equivalents. **Source** (p.113)

- **RULE** Highlight awards, certifications and work for known companies prominently on resume/portfolio.
  **Why** They raise credibility and make you the first pick among similar candidates.
  **Applies to** landing page (portfolio proof). **Check** visual: proof element above the fold. **Source** (p.26) (opinion)

- **RULE** Prepare to explain your design process and the idea behind a project, and why you chose a method; also be able to explain design choices to non-designers (why a persona, why more testing, why a layout is better).
  **Why** Interviews and clients ask for the reasoning, not only the output.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** each major decision has a written rationale. **Source** (p.14-15, 112-118) [engine note: an agent should attach a short "why" to each design decision it ships]

- **RULE** Keep written messages "short and specific"; reread before sending; use spellcheck but do not rely on it.
  **Why** Intended meaning must survive transmission.
  **Applies to** all outbound text. **Check** manual. **Source** (p.17-18)

- **RULE** Keep the portfolio updated with new finished work.
  **Why** "How quickly you get work depends on how unique and experienced your profile is"; new projects fix a thin profile (p.54-56).
  **Applies to** landing page (portfolio). **Check** latest entry date. **Source** (p.55-56)

### Audit additions (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Lay out a case-study page in the order the book's example shows: a hero with client mark, project title and a one-line summary beside product renders; then a short narrative section led by a plain-language headline ("Everybody knows how to chat.") with one supporting illustration; then a two-column block in a tinted panel with "My role" (a short paragraph) on the left and "What I did" (a bullet list of activities) on the right; then process sections with real screens and captions ("Leveraging what we've learned").
  **Why** The role and activity list answer the reader's first question (what did this person do) before the process detail, which carries the five case-study questions (p.113 list).
  **Applies to** landing page (portfolio and case-study templates). **Check** case-study page has a hero with title + summary, and a "My role"/"What I did" pair above the first process section; visual. **Source** (p.113 figure) (audit 2026-10-15)

### Audit additions: page images (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Use your success stories (past project outcomes) as the main material for winning new work, and build "a beautiful and informative professional profile" that lists every important quality you have.
  **Why** The Experience section ends with this as its last practical point (p.46); the platform chapter closes with the same advice for marketplace profiles (p.42).
  **Applies to** landing page (portfolio, agency and case-study pages). **Check** portfolio/landing page leads with concrete outcomes (project, result) rather than a skills list; visual. **Source** (p.42, p.46) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** For pages people read for long periods (listings, dashboards, docs), avoid a small body font and avoid vivid, saturated colour across large surfaces.
  **Why** The book's own reviews of job boards: Ideate Labs "can be difficult to see for long periods of time owing to the small font"; Design Jobs Board "might get visually heavy after a time" because of its vivid colours (the screenshot shows full-width cyan and teal row fills) (p.96-97).
  **Applies to** SaaS app UI, long-form and listing pages. **Check** body `font-size` >= 14px (see dos-and-donts T86); saturated fills limited to accents, not whole rows or panels; visual. **Source** (p.96-97, screenshots p.97) (audit 2026-10-15)

## 4. Dos and Don'ts

- Do: show an app as a final mock-up on a phone (p.20). Don't: show a bare artboard.
- Do (resume p.105 figure): plain monochrome single-column CV with left label column, role entries, education and a skills row. Don't: bright colours, striking shapes, unconventional layout.
- Do: "How I solved X, what it took" Don't: "I designed the navigation system" (p.107).
- Do: lead with achievements and what you learned. Don't: list tasks (p.111).
- Red flags on a job listing (diagram p.103): excess of requested tasks; unrealistic and excessive requirements; absurd tasks; lack of diversity in the company; unrealistic wages; department to which the UX team reports.

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls

- The "don't overdesign" rule is for a CV addressed to a screening recruiter. A designer's own portfolio site is a different brief; the book does not say how bold a portfolio site should be. [engine note: restraint rule applies to reading-first documents; marketing/brand surfaces follow their own direction.]
- Whether a social-showcase portfolio (Dribbble/Behance) is a "red flag" for an employer is called a "common red flag for many designers"; the book then says it depends on personal preference (p.102-104). Not a hard rule.
- Reporting line (marketing director vs head developer) as a red flag is explicitly personal preference (p.103).
- Tailoring (p.108): "no one has time to develop a new resume for each application"; only do it for the golden listing.
- Interview "Design Thinking" question (p.116): the book says it's fine to critique Agile/Lean if reasoned.
