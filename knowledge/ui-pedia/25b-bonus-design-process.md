# Bonus B: The UI/UX design process, and book close

Second half of the Bonus chapter (PDF 615-626). The practice exercises (584-615) are in 25a. This part is the author's own five-stage process ("every brain functions in a unique way ... the steps I'm mentioning are mine", p.615-616). It is generic UX-process description with no numbers except the stage count. For the engine it matters as a pipeline skeleton and a final review checklist.

## 1. Core ideas
1. Every brain works differently; don't mindlessly follow anyone's routine, but creativity is the binding trait and practice plus knowledge generate ideas ("you can't make something on your own if you don't know anything about it", p.615-616). The author makes no claim to be the best UI designer (opinion, p.616).
2. UI/UX design determines whether a product will succeed; best approach combines structure, content and user experience so the business reaches its objectives (p.616).
3. Take a user-centred approach: it lets you create "micro-moments" that are straightforward, intuitive and engaging (p.616).
4. Without a process you end up remodelling regularly (p.617).
5. Five stages in sequence: **Product Definition -> Research -> Analysis -> Design -> Validation**, with a feedback loop from Validation back to Product Definition (p.617 figure: five circles joined by arrows; a dashed line returns from Validation to Product Definition; Product Definition circle is darker and separated from the rest by a vertical dashed divider).
6. Every stage is scrutinised by the responsible department, so the result will be "nearly perfect" (p.617, opinion).

## 2. Rules

- **RULE** Stage 1, Product Definition: gather user needs in the business context and inform UI/UX designers of the requirements **before** work begins. Design Team, Business Manager and Product Manager are all active; the whole team consults clients in their own surroundings; examine requirements within the context of the business.
  **Why** This is when "the true extent of the product and its existence is realised". **Applies to** all. **Check** a written brief with requirements exists before design starts. **Source** (p.618)
- **RULE** Product Definition deliverables: **User Personas, User Stories, Use Case Diagrams.**
  **Why** They fix who the users are and what they do. **Applies to** SaaS app UI | forms. **Check** the three artifacts exist. **Source** (p.618)
- **RULE** Stage 2, Research (called "the most important aspect" for a designer): investigate how the current system functions for the customer proposal. Three primary functions: know what the competition is about; examine your current domain thoroughly; examine competitor strategy "to see what happens". Include the newest UI/UX trends, design principles and guidelines.
  **Why** Design decisions need market and domain grounding. **Applies to** all. **Check** competitor and domain notes exist. **Source** (p.619)
- **RULE** Stage 3, Analysis: use the research to create **hypothetical personas** (to understand the types of people who will use the product and allow an accurate portrayal of the final product) and **user experience maps** (depict the flow of users through the final product, via visual representations and correct interactions with clients during product definition).
  **Why** Turns raw research into usable models. **Applies to** all. **Check** personas and a user flow/experience map exist. **Source** (p.620)
- **RULE** Stage 4, Design: this is where final graphics begin. Key outcomes, in order: **Sketching** (hand-drawn sketches to visualise concepts simply, then pick one option); **Wireframes** (visual representation of page hierarchy and product aspects, the "backbone"/"skeleton" of the design, largely about overall appearance); **Prototypes** (look and feel and interaction experience, "similar to that of a simulator"); **Design specifications** (user flow and task flow diagrams, the overall working and stylistic requirements, procedures and graphical aspects).
  **Why** Each step narrows from idea to buildable detail. **Applies to** all. **Check** sketch, wireframe, prototype and spec artifacts. **Source** (p.621-623)
- **RULE** Stage 5, Validation and Testing: testers list items needing improvement and send them back to the responsible team to fix. Distinguish **User Testing ("Do users need my app?")** from **Usability Testing ("Can users use my app?")**.
  **Why** They answer two different questions: demand vs ease of use. **Applies to** all. **Check** both questions were asked. **Source** (p.622 figure, p.622-623 text)
- **RULE** Final review checklist, four questions: Is the system **simple to use**? Is it **adaptable and simple to use** (sic; OCR/image both read this, apparently meaning adaptable/flexible)? Is it **effective in resolving the customer's problem**? Is it **trustworthy, and does it entice users to return** every time they need your services?
  **Why** Covers ease, flexibility, effectiveness, and trust/retention. **Applies to** all. **Check** answer all four per delivery. **Source** (p.623)
- **RULE** Use a systematic, planned strategy (a UI/UX design process plan) to produce excellent interfaces.
  **Why** Avoids repeated remodelling. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.617, p.623)

## 4. Dos and Don'ts
- Do define requirements with the whole team and client before design starts; don't start design from an assumed brief (p.618).
- Do sketch before wireframing and pick one option (p.621); don't jump to final graphics.
- Figure p.619 (printed 618): a use-case diagram: one actor on the left connected by blue lines to four oval use cases (stacked) and to a fifth that links onward to another actor; a third actor at top right connected to the two top ovals. Shows who does which task.
- Figure p.621 (printed 620): hand-sketch style wireframe: a phone screen with hamburger, search field, user and cart icons, image carousel with arrows and dots, heart, title/text lines, colour dropdown, quantity stepper and ADD TO TROLLEY button; beside it a kit of sketched components: nav bar, search field, checkbox/radio/dropdown, button, two toggles, slider, an icon row (image, search, user, cart, bin, pencil, pin) and a second row (arrows, chevrons, plus, minus, x, star, heart, thumbs up, mail, download).
- Figure p.622 (printed 621): two panels. "User Testing / Do Users Need My App..?": four user avatars with marks (tick, cross, tick, tick from left to right). "Usability Testing / Can Users Use my App..?": a person with a laptop and a "We're hiring" sign-holder (illustrative).

- **Do** (p.621 wireframe figure) keep the wireframe greyscale line art and spend a single accent colour (blue) only on what carries hierarchy or interaction: the title bar line in the content block, the slider knob and the dropdown chevron. Everything else (image placeholder, text lines, buttons, icons) stays outline-only. (audit 2026-10-15)
- **Do** (p.617 process figure) the first stage, Product Definition, is drawn darker and separated from the other four by a vertical dashed divider, marking it as the client-facing setup stage before the research-to-validation loop. (audit 2026-10-15)

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls
- The author says the process is personal and brains differ (p.615-616): treat as one valid process, not the only one (opinion).
- "Research" and "Analysis" claims "hypothetical personas" are built from research; no method (interviews, sample size) is given. The book offers no numbers for testing (e.g. how many users).
- Prototype definition is loose: "look and feel ... experience of interacting" (p.622), while the Hard Skills chapter (24) defines prototyping as testing functionality. Both are in the book; the engine should treat a prototype as an interactive simulation.
- [engine note] The feedback loop from Validation back to Product Definition (p.617 figure) is the book's only explicit statement that the process is iterative, not waterfall. Map it to the engine's ledger/eval loop; this mapping is not in the book.
- [engine note] For a code-first engine, "wireframe" and "prototype" collapse into one render; keep the sketch/hierarchy step as a layout plan before styling.
