# Illustrations

Page numbering: all (p.NN) are PDF pages (printed page = PDF page - 1). This chapter is almost entirely qualitative. It gives no pixel sizes, ratios, colour values or durations. The figures are screenshots of real brand sites (Duolingo, Mailchimp, Slack, ReadMe, Moonfarmer, Pitch, GitHub, Trello, TunnelBear, HubSpot, Salesforce), so the lessons come from what those sites do, not from numeric rules. The cover page (p.479) promises "Types of illustrations", "What are good illustrations?" and "How to know what type of illustration suits your design?". The chapter never actually answers the last two as stated (see section 7).

## 1. Core ideas

1. **An illustration is functional, not decoration.** The book defines it as the visual representation of a story that visualises a function, procedure or environment, and says that on websites and apps it is "a functional element, despite a decoration". It differs from pure art because it has an object of interaction (p.480, p.482).
2. **Before adding one, analyse the audience.** The illustration must help users navigate, carry the product's tone and accomplish their goals (p.482).
3. **Illustrations are read faster than text.** This suits skimmers, who skip text and rely on images, and it suits limited screen real estate (mobile, mobile-first, tutorials, tooltips) (p.481, p.487).
4. **The book names seven UI roles:** theme/mood, rewards, onboarding, tutorials and pop-ups, entertainment, mascots, and explaining concepts such as pricing tiers, error meaning and plan contents (p.482-484, p.486).
5. **Its four-point framework:**
   - (1) get the main point across faster than text;
   - (2) engage the user through their journey;
   - (3) create dynamic brand experiences;
   - (4) strengthen the aesthetic appeal (p.486-492, p.500).
6. **Style consistency with the brand matters.** Mailchimp's hand-sketch onboarding illustrations are praised because "the art style is consistent with the brand and simple to understand" (p.489). TunnelBear's bear is "unique, memorable, and consistent with the brand" (p.498).
7. **Light animation makes illustrations memorable.** Examples are ReadMe's animated hero graphic and its owl mascot that closes its eyes when you type a password (p.491).
8. **Mascots carry brand memory and voice.** Mascots are personified characters that add personification to the UI, act as communicators between interface and user, and support the voice and tone of the site (p.484, p.492-493).
9. **Illustrations create theme and mood on first contact.** A distinct mood is felt at first engagement, and visual metaphors plus brand connotations are set quickly (p.493-494).
10. **Illustration can lead the eye through a narrative.** HubSpot's style guide says illustration is used to lead visitors to a certain point in the narrative (p.499).

## 2. Rules

- **RULE** Give every illustration a job (explain, guide, reward, set mood, represent brand). Do not add one as pure decoration. Run the audience check first: does it help users navigate, convey the product's tone, or accomplish their goals?
  **Why** The book frames UI illustration as a functional element that serves an interaction goal, unlike pure art.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** visual (each illustration maps to one named role from the list in section 3). **Source** (p.480, p.482)

- **RULE** Use an illustration to carry the main point of a step, concept or state faster than copy would. It is most useful for pricing tiers, what an error message means, and what a plan includes, and for explaining how to do something.
  **Why** Visual elements are perceived more quickly than words and work for skimmers who skip text.
  **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI | mobile. **Check** visual: remove the text and see whether the point is still guessable. **Source** (p.486-487)

- **RULE** On mobile, or any layout with scarce space, prefer a small illustration over a text block for tutorials and tooltips.
  **Why** The book says illustrations work well for limited screen real estate and in tutorials and tooltips, where they clarify a step or inspire action.
  **Applies to** mobile. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.484, p.487)

- **RULE** Check every detail of an illustration used in a tutorial or pop-up so that it cannot be misread.
  **Why** A visual prompt with a wrong or ambiguous detail causes misunderstanding. The book says to ensure "every detail of the picture is well-checked to keep misunderstandings apart".
  **Applies to** tutorials, onboarding, pop-ups. **Check** visual review. **Source** (p.484)

- **RULE** Use illustrations in onboarding and tutorials to demonstrate how to do something. For long or complicated flows, put one illustration per step.
  **Why** It keeps the user interested and makes a long process easier. The book calls this "very helpful for creating long or complicated onboarding processes".
  **Applies to** SaaS app UI | mobile | forms (multi-step). **Check** count of onboarding steps versus illustrations (1:1 in the Mailchimp example). **Source** (p.488-489)

- **RULE** Keep a single illustration style across all assets: same line treatment and the same character language.
  **Why** The book approves illustrations that are "consistent with the brand and simple to understand" and that build "artistic harmony and originality". Mailchimp's four onboarding steps all use the same black ink-sketch style with yellow accents. TunnelBear uses one bear character everywhere.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.485, p.489, p.498)

- **RULE** Use one recurring character or mascot to represent the brand, and use it across the product (features, product names, price, help). Give it a trait that is mentioned in brand guidance, for example Freddie always winks.
  **Why** Well-designed mascots increase brand memory, and a character is recognisable without a logo. Examples are GitHub's Octocat, Trello's husky Taco, Mailchimp's Freddie, TunnelBear's bear, and Salesforce's Astro and Codey.
  **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI. **Check** visual: the character appears in more than one context. **Source** (p.484, p.492-498, p.500)

- **RULE** Use a mascot for contextual help inside the product, not as the logo. Trello's husky Taco appears occasionally "to provide contextual help and isn't a logo".
  **Why** It keeps the logo clean while the character carries voice and tone.
  **Applies to** SaaS app UI. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.496)

- **RULE** Add a small motion to illustrations that carry brand or interaction state, such as an animated hero graphic or a mascot that reacts to input. Keep it simple.
  **Why** "Simple animation effects on illustrations" improve interaction and make brand experiences memorable and dynamic. ReadMe's owl closes its eyes while you type a password.
  **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI. **Check** visual, and motion present on at most the illustration, not the whole page. **Source** (p.491)

- **RULE** Use simple artwork for rewards and progress: cups, stars, medals, stickers, badges, awards and trophies.
  **Why** Simple artwork "makes the achievement feel even greater" and adds gamification.
  **Applies to** SaaS app UI | mobile. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.483, p.492)

- **RULE** Use illustration as the hero/theme device when a brand's mood is the product. Pair the artwork with brand-matching microcopy so the page is memorable.
  **Why** Theme illustrations quickly present visual metaphors and establish strong brand connotations. The book shows Moonfarmer (dark navy night scene, astronaut gardener, spaced capitals wordmark), a Denver skyline in light blues with a white tagline, and Pitch (olive-brown top band, off-white lower scene, orange accents, line-art campsite).
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.482-483, p.492-494)

- **RULE** An illustration may simply entertain. Use it to get attention without forcing an action, and aim it at the aesthetic and emotional side of the interaction rather than usability.
  **Why** It engages and can still carry a brand message in a unique way (HubSpot, Salesforce hang-gliding mascots).
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.484, p.498-500)

- **RULE** When an illustration shows the product (Slack, Mailchimp), pair it with the headline and a few words of copy. The illustration invites a click-through, it does not replace the explanation.
  **Why** Slack uses drawings to show essential capabilities "at a glance" and "invites them to click through to learn more". The illustration accompanies a heading, one short paragraph and a few bullets.
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.489-490)

- **RULE** Use illustrations to signal trust or values on dedicated pages (for example a security page) when the topic is abstract.
  **Why** Slack's "Security at Slack" page uses a full-width flat illustration of people at a network diagram with padlock/shield/check nodes to show the team cares about security.
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.490)

### Audit additions (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Use an illustration to steer attention: place it so it strengthens the visual hierarchy and pulls the eye to a specific screen zone, an essential detail, or the key message.
  **Why** The book lists improving visual hierarchy and engaging users "towards screen zones, essential details, or visual messages" as benefits, alongside activating the psychology of shapes, colours and visual metaphors.
  **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI. **Check** visual: the illustration sits beside or points toward the primary message or CTA, not in an unrelated zone. **Source** (p.481) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Lead the eye through a narrative by sequencing illustrated scenes in reading order toward the point you want. HubSpot's hero runs, from left to right away from the copy, a small house, a mid-rise shop and a tall office building, so the illustration itself tells "grow".
  **Why** HubSpot's style guide (as cited) says illustration is used "to lead visitors, users, or other viewers to a certain point in the narrative".
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual: scene elements progress in size or state along the reading direction. **Source** (p.499) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** When the product is the story, put real product UI inside the illustrated world rather than replacing it. Salesforce sets real dashboard screenshots on a laptop, tablet and phone inside an illustrated landscape with the mascot hang-gliding above; Trello's hero shows illustrated people (and the husky) working at abstracted product boards.
  **Why** The illustration carries mood and brand while the screenshot carries proof of the product.
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual: hero illustration contains a recognisable product surface. **Source** (p.497, p.500) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Re-stage the same mascot in the target segment's own setting to signal fit: TunnelBear's teams page puts the bears in office cubicles, at a water cooler and a photocopier, so visitors "see right away" that the product suits groups.
  **Why** Same character keeps brand memory; the new setting carries the segment message.
  **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual: segment pages reuse the mascot in a segment-specific scene. **Source** (p.498) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Treat the mascot as a brand asset separate from the logo: GitHub publishes three distinct assets (wordmark logo, mark, Octocat), and the Octocat appears in many costumes and poses (six variants shown) while its silhouette stays recognisable. Mailchimp always pairs the company name with the Freddie icon and keeps both a stacked and a horizontal lockup.
  **Why** Separation lets the mascot flex in tone and context without weakening the logo.
  **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI (brand assets). **Check** brand kit lists logo, mark and mascot as separate files; mascot variants keep the same head/body silhouette. **Source** (p.495-496) (audit 2026-10-15)

## 4. Dos and Don'ts

There are no explicit good/bad pairs in this chapter. Everything is a positive example. Implicit contrasts:

- **Do** use a consistent hand-drawn style across a multi-step onboarding (Mailchimp: four panels, each with the step title on the left and an ink illustration on the right; the last panel adds yellow ribbon and a "SEND!" tag as a reward moment) (p.489).
- **Do** use a character that stays recognisable at logo size and large (Mailchimp Freddie winking, in a black-on-grey logo lockup and elsewhere in a black bird on yellow hero) (p.495).
- **Do** place illustration beside a short headline and CTA in the hero (Trello: left text block "Organize anything, with anyone" plus green "Get Started - It's Free!", right illustration of people at boards; HubSpot: heading plus white CTA left, buildings and people right) (p.497, p.499).
- **Don't** use illustrations purely as filler without checking the audience or purpose (implied by "functional element despite a decoration") (p.482).
- **Don't** use an ambiguous detail in a tutorial illustration (p.484).

- **Do** (p.491) ReadMe hero: centred small headline "Say Goodbye to Static Documentation" with an outlined button below it, then an animated graphic of stacked documentation cards (red centre card with "yourlogo", side cards in green and yellow), then a row of customer logos on white beneath the blue hero. The illustration sits between the claim and the proof row. (audit 2026-10-15)
- **Do** (p.499) HubSpot: left-aligned white headline, short paragraph and a white rectangular CTA on a teal-to-green hero; illustration of buildings growing in size fills the right two-thirds. (audit 2026-10-15)
- **Do** (p.500) Salesforce: left vertical menu panel ("Solutions for", "Our products") over the illustrated sky-and-hills scene; two small dark buttons under a light headline; real product screenshots on devices sit in the landscape. (audit 2026-10-15)
- **Do** (p.498) TunnelBear: centred serif headline, one dark rectangular CTA ("Get Started, It's Free" / "Try free for 7 days"), mascot scene directly below the CTA on a pale aqua field. (audit 2026-10-15)

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls

- The book says entertainment illustration "is not about usability or functionality", which sits oddly next to its "functional element" claim. Resolve by role: a hero or entertainment piece is allowed to be emotional only, but it should still be directed at a brand message (p.484, p.498-499).
- The book lists many generic benefits (humour, "illusion of real communication", "strengthen brand recognition") without evidence or measurable criteria. Treat them as opinion (p.485).
- The "Duolingo" example (p.487-488) is cited for illustrated tooltips. The figure shows a green owl in the bottom-left corner of a lesson screen with a speech bubble whose text reads "Remember that you can always hover over words to see what they mean". The point is that the mascot delivers the hint, not that a hover feature exists. The caption text also mentions "animated drawings" (opinion/claim, not visible in the still).
- [engine note] Illustration needs real style governance. Mixing stock-looking 3D, flat vector and photos breaks the "consistent with the brand" rule that all examples depend on. A generator should pick one illustration style per project and reuse it.
- [engine note] Mascots only work when the brand has the volume to repeat them. A one-page site should not invent a mascot just because this chapter lists them.
