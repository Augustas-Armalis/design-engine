# Bonus A: Exercises to improve UI design skills

The Bonus chapter (PDF 584-626) turns out to have two parts: (A) five weekly practice exercises, credited in part to Erik D. Kennedy (the "copywork" example is explicitly "taken from the idea given by Erik D. Kennedy", p.598; the style tile story is first person, "older/wiser designer-Erik", p.608) and (B) the author's 5-stage UI/UX process (see 25b). This note covers (A), PDF 584-615.

It is a learning method, not a rule set. The design lessons it embeds (pastel palette, grid-breaking, quirky type, brand in every element, huge type, paint-stroke shadows, tracked uppercase, 60/30/10 palette, "anything but blue" for finance, cards neat) are captured as rules below, each labelled as an observation from a specific example (opinion, not law).

## 1. Core ideas
1. Information overload is the main problem when learning design: articles are short, single-topic crumbs; this chapter is the "hub of a wheel" (p.585-586).
2. **90% production, 10% consumption.** Spend 10 minutes designing for every 1 minute reading design articles (p.586, p.588). Reading is a thousand times easier than designing but designing is far more important to ability.
3. Parable (p.586-587): a friend admires each screw while building a climbing wall instead of drilling; consuming/dreaming is easier than the work, so people read, discuss and browse endlessly instead of making.
4. Exercises beat reading because they point you at the right question; "you have to fully immerse yourself in the action before you can tell where you're going to have trouble" (p.588-589). Example: color theory reading was worthless to the author until the real question arose, "I have a button that seems too plain, what options do I have?" (p.589).
5. "Gut instinct" is not magic: it is an unconscious map of what options exist and which work better, built from what you have consciously studied; sketch the map by recording why things work (p.589-591, p.595-596).
6. Gaps in taste are normal for beginners (p.589).
7. Fast loop wins: generate idea -> put into action -> assess; the shorter this cycle, the faster you learn (p.609).
8. Exercises also produce reusable assets (lists, resources, fonts database), "earning compound interest on your time" (p.587).
9. Five exercises, each with a weekly assignment: (1) analyse good and bad UI, (2) copywork, (3) fonts database, (4) style tiles, (5) personal project mockups (p.589, p.595, p.600-601, p.604, p.610-614).

## 2. Rules

### Exercise 1: analyse your gut instinct (p.589-595)
- **RULE** Keep a list of apps/sites with outstanding UI and some with awful UI, and write down specifically why for each.
  **Why** Making the unconscious map explicit turns taste into reusable principles. **Applies to** all. **Check** process: list exists with a reason per entry. **Source** (p.591)
- **RULE** Weekly: list **5 good-UI sites/apps and 1-2 bad-UI ones**, with the explanation in your own words.
  **Why** It trains judgement of what works and what does not. **Applies to** all. **Check** count = 5 good + 1-2 bad. **Source** (p.595)
- **RULE** When collecting references, stay in your own field (e.g. a product designer working on data-heavy UI should seek data-heavy UI references).
  **Why** Context-matched examples transfer; others do not. **Applies to** all. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.592; OCR on that page is garbled around this sentence, wording is from image: "it's important to note though, that in this exercise you should seek references from your field of work. If you're a product designer working with data-heavy UI.")
- **RULE** Agreement with the author is not the point; understanding why you feel the way you do is. Generalise each observation into a design philosophy you can reuse.
  **Why** Principles generalise, opinions do not. **Applies to** all. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.593)

Worked observations from the example, labelled as the author's taste (opinion):
- **RULE** Pastel colour schemes feel more relaxing than dark/saturated ones. Example figure on p.593: same hue pairs, only saturation and brightness changed (caption on p.594 "Each of these swatches has the same hue between devices - it's merely the saturation and brightness that have changed"). (opinion)
  **Why** Lower saturation/higher lightness reduces visual intensity. **Applies to** landing page | mobile. **Check** compare S and L in HSL/OKLCH between candidate palettes. **Source** (p.593-594)
- **RULE** Add playfulness with randomness and "grid-breaking" in the layout (overlapping a card-like image over a dark panel; "scattered assortment of books lend playful feel"). (opinion)
  **Why** Deliberate irregularity reads as whimsical. **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual: some elements overlap or break the column grid. **Source** (p.594)
- **RULE** Sans-serif fonts with unusual stroke weights or kerning give a slightly untamed look; an old, imperfectly-spaced Century Schoolbook gives "quirky charm" fitting a studio's vibe (MSDS Studio example). (opinion)
  **Why** Imperfect type carries personality. **Applies to** landing page (brand/portfolio). **Check** visual. **Source** (p.594-595)
- **RULE** Infuse features of the brand into all elements: logo, layout, colours and so on. (opinion)
  **Why** Consistency across elements makes the brand coherent (the Abstract example: logo, name and scattered shapes support the brand). **Applies to** all. **Check** visual: the same shape/colour motifs recur in logo, layout and graphics. **Source** (p.593, p.595)
- Abstract site observations (p.592-593): pastel palette is enjoyable and relaxing; a haphazard sprinkling of rounded rectangles has a whimsical sense; the typeface "Euclid Circular B" gives a clean, plain vibe (OCR "Euclid Circulor B"; image reads "Euclid Circular B"); brand supported by logo, name and scattering of shapes.

### Exercise 2: copywork (p.596-601)
- **RULE** Reproduce an excellent existing design pixel-for-pixel in your design tool.
  **Why** It forces you to redo every decision (typeface, size, layout, graphics, backgrounds, decoration), exposing choices you would not have made and tricks you would not have noticed by looking. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.596, p.598)
- **RULE** Choose what to copy by what you want to improve: insane gradients or a bright palette for colour use; a prestigious "ritzy" site for premium branding; or copy very specific parts.
  **Why** Targeted copying fills known gaps. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.601)
- **RULE** Weekly: copy the UI of a decent site/app pixel-for-pixel, then write down the particular approaches the author used "to increase your design vocabulary". May be done as a **30-minute daily activity** instead of all at once.
  **Why** Writing the techniques down converts copied pixels into transferable vocabulary. **Applies to** all. **Check** a written list of techniques exists. **Source** (p.601)
- Historical precedent cited to legitimise copywork: Jack London copying Kipling's passages, Robert Louis Stevenson reciting lovely passages from memory, Ben Franklin taking notes on each sentence then rebuilding the essay days later, Leonardo da Vinci prescribing reproducing a good master's drawings first (p.597). Copywork is now seen as "routine, unimaginative, and plagiarised" (p.597-598); the author expects a reversal (opinion).
- Observations from copying Dann Petty's Oahu Epicurrence site (the book gives these as "what I noticed in the header alone"), each a design technique to keep:
  - **RULE** Font size can be absurdly huge when used as a visual element: the "HI" letters were **size 365** in the copy; the large text is aligned with other header elements, with an image inserted in the space between the letters. (opinion) **Why** Oversized type becomes a graphic, not just a label. **Applies to** landing page (hero). **Check** computed font-size of hero display text vs viewport; visual. **Source** (p.599-600)
  - **RULE** Use a paint stroke / watercolour smudge as the "shadow": under the seal, the header and the pineapple, where a shadow would normally go, as if painted on the page. (opinion) **Why** A painted smudge gives depth with brand character instead of a generic drop shadow. **Applies to** landing page. **Check** visual. **Source** (p.600)
  - **RULE** Letter-spacing is generous in uppercase type; all text on the site is capitalised and almost all has altered letter-spacing. (opinion) **Why** Tracked uppercase gives a strong sense of alignment and "classiness" (the author notes he feels self-conscious doing it often). **Applies to** landing page. **Check** `text-transform: uppercase` elements have positive `letter-spacing`. **Source** (p.600)
- Meta-lesson: the author had seen the design and thought it looked fantastic but did not internalise its techniques until replicating it (p.600-601).

### Exercise 3: fonts database (p.601-605)
- **RULE** Build a personal list of good fonts organically as you meet them in real sites/apps, not by Googling "best fonts" lists.
  **Why** Lists with more than about 10 recommendations drift into niche-use or sub-par typefaces. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.602-603)
- **RULE** For each font record: **font name; website/app it was found on; usages it would be good for (body font, title font, certain types of brands, etc.); a screenshot of it in use (especially if you cannot download it); price.**
  **Why** Re-findable, usage-tagged knowledge makes selection fast later. **Applies to** all. **Check** each entry has those 5 fields. **Source** (p.603)
- **RULE** Organise fonts in a font manager (FontBase, cross-platform; or Font Book on Mac) into groups such as "Brush" or "Chunky heavyweights"; use multiple groupings for different font types and usage concepts.
  **Why** Quick comparison and retrieval. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.604)
- **RULE** If a font you like is free, download it immediately; keep fonts on hand and compare a paragraph of body text in font A vs font B as quickly as possible.
  **Why** You must try a font to know if it fits; you will not want to download a thousand fonts noted last year at project start. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.604-605)
- Claim: "web design is 90% typography" may be an exaggeration but typography is an important talent; choosing from thousands of fonts is the easy part, styling them correctly is "far more difficult - and much more important" (p.602). Weekly: list fonts you enjoy, with when they work and where you saw them (p.605).

### Exercise 4: style tiles (p.605-611)
- **RULE** Use style tiles: small artboards showing the brand aspects under consideration (font, colour, form controls, logo). They preview the feel, not the final product, and are brief one-off explorations of one UI concept.
  **Why** Cheap, concrete, debatable. A client and designer can say what is working and what is not "from a notion like this" instead of talking about brand in broad terms. **Applies to** landing page | SaaS app UI (brand direction). **Check** artifact exists. **Source** (p.605-609)
- Style tile ingredients from the author's finance example (p.607-609):
  - **Logo**: for a first iteration just make a simple text wordmark (author tried about "a third of a dozen" = 4 typeface/style options, settled on Museo bold, a quirky sans that resembles a serif when small). (opinion: "for beginners, it'll suffice")
  - **Colors**: blue is the dull, predictable bank colour; the brief was "anything but blue". The author picked colours of **euro bills** because they read as "financial/money" yet are not blue. Logos of Merrill Lynch, Prudential, Barclays, Bank of America, Citi, Chase were shown as the blue-heavy pattern to avoid.
  - **Snippets of UI elements**: show a sample of items that may appear on the site (stock chart, user-submitted stock-tip card), not a full style guide. A button is typically one of the first things put on a style tile. **"Cards should be neat"** (the author regrets a dirty tan card colour).
  - **Font**: PT Sans, called a high-quality, free, underutilised Google Font.
  - **Copy snippet**: use real language that could appear on the site (e.g. "3 clicks and no hidden fees", a casual user-submitted stock tip) because how the site communicates is part of the brand.
- **RULE** The brief behind the example: finance platform where people feel comfortable depositing large sums, but "not anything too generic", must not look as tech-illiterate as a typical bank, must stand out (p.605).
  **Why** Trust plus differentiation is the design tension for fintech. **Applies to** landing page (fintech). **Check** visual. **Source** (p.605)
- **RULE** Practise style tiles as a personal exercise: low-risk, quick, easy to try; no approval, UX limits, budget or bureaucracy. Example recipe: "perfect for a premium brand" font + classy Unsplash photographs + grayscale + gold, then play.
  **Why** Fast idea/implement/assess cycles accelerate learning. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.609-610)
- **RULE** Weekly: create **2-3 style tiles** for fonts in your database. Think of a site concept, then design a tile with: realistic font choices and text elements (headers, navigation, body text); an appropriate colour palette; example form controls or imagery; a logo (optional).
  **Why** Applies the font collection in context. **Applies to** all. **Check** tile contains those 3 required + 1 optional items. **Source** (p.611)
- Colour proportion figure (p.608): a bar labelled **60% Primary Colour, 30% Secondary Colour, 10% Accent Colour** (dark slate, periwinkle blue, light sky blue), captioned "Thinking outside the box for color palette inspiration" and introduced by "I notice pattern here" about the bank logos. The text does not name it the 60-30-10 rule or give application guidance; it is shown as the proportion pattern in the palette.

### Exercise 5: personal project mockups (p.611-615)
- **RULE** Do personal projects: unpaid, with you alone having complete creative control. Forms: build an app you wish existed; remodel an existing app/site you believe could be better; create a site for a non-profit/charity (you may send it to them).
  **Why** Removes real-world constraints so you learn faster. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.611-612)
- **RULE** Choose a project whose success criterion is "do I think it looks beautiful?" rather than one with a partner's heavy feedback.
  **Why** With a partner you must also understand their feedback and converge on something both like, much harder for a beginner. The author notes (humorously) that the real world is not like this; it is a learning exercise. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.612)
- **RULE** A good starting point: take a style tile you enjoyed and turn it into a fuller app/site.
  **Why** Builds on work already done. **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.613)
- Three scope levels, deciding how much design to finish before diminishing returns (p.613-614):
  1. **Self-study project**: only for your education; may skip much of UX and real-world business limits; focus on visual skills (colour, font). Design the **primary screens** only: homepage, profiles, dashboards, feeds, articles (where users spend most time).
  2. **Portfolio project**: design the entire app, including auxiliary experiences: **error states, loading pages, first-time-use flows**, etc.
  3. **Real app/site**: if you or a partner can develop, it may become real; at that point "graduate to a real curriculum".
- **RULE** Weekly: design **1-3 screens** of the app/site you most want to work on using the style tiles from Exercise 4.
  **Why** Ties together noted design details, lessons from copywork, collected fonts and styles. **Applies to** all. **Check** 1-3 screens exist. **Source** (p.614-615)
- Closing: practise each exercise weekly; practice is the key (p.615).

### Audit additions (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Spend more effort styling type (size, weight, leading, letter-spacing, case) than choosing the typeface: picking from thousands of fonts is "the easy part"; styling them correctly is "far more difficult - and much more important".
  **Why** A good face set badly still reads as amateur; the book ranks styling above selection.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** visual: every text style has a deliberate size/leading/tracking tuple, not tool defaults. **Source** (p.602) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** When exploring a direction and the ideal font is too expensive, test it with "a nice free replacement" rather than dropping the idea; keep candidate fonts installed so you can compare a paragraph of body text in font A vs font B at once.
  **Why** You only know if a font fits by trying it in place; cost should not block the experiment.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.604-605, p.610) (audit 2026-10-15)

- **RULE** Show self-study work to more experienced designers for comments even when it is not meant for anyone else.
  **Why** The self-study project skips real-world constraints, so outside critique is the remaining feedback loop.
  **Applies to** all. **Check** process. **Source** (p.613) (audit 2026-10-15)

## 4. Dos and Don'ts
- Pastel vs dark/saturated (p.593 figure, caption p.594): two phone mockups each with three tiles "Get Discount On 32% / 45% / 70%". Left phone: muted slate-blue, periwinkle, light sky blue (pastel, relaxed). Right phone: dark navy, strong royal blue, steel blue (dark/saturated, heavier). Same hues; only saturation and brightness differ. Lesson: pastel reads relaxed.
- Neat vs dirty cards (p.608 text): the author's "dirty tan" card colour is the example of what to avoid; "cards should be neat".
- Blue-by-default finance (p.607-608): six financial logos all blue = predictable; choosing euro-note colours is the differentiator. Do not copy the category default if the brief says stand out.
- Real-vs-learning constraint: personal project must be judged by "looks beautiful", not by pleasing a partner (p.612).

- **Do** (p.594 grid-breaking figure) stack a headline in mixed colour and size per line: "YOUR SUCCESS" in slate-blue caps, "IS OUR" smaller in black caps, "BUSINESS." larger in black caps; on the right an iridescent image card overlaps the left edge of a black rounded panel (about a third of the card hangs outside the panel) and casts a soft coloured glow. (audit 2026-10-15)
- **Do** (p.606 style tile mock) small details worth keeping: text-link nav followed by one filled periwinkle "Login" button and a black square icon button; a "01/12" slide counter flanked by thin rules under the hero copy; cards with a small grey category label above a two-line title, a short description and a hairline rule beneath; a vertical rotated side label "Pixselacademy UI Book" along the right edge. (audit 2026-10-15)

## 5. Exceptions & judgement calls
- Every design "rule" here comes from one example and is the author's taste ("I'd explain why in the following way"); the book explicitly says readers may appreciate the same design for different reasons (p.592). Do not hard-code them as universal rules.
- Pastel > saturated for relaxation conflicts with the finance brief (trust/stand out) only in degree; the author chose a non-blue palette, not a pastel one. Different goals pick different saturation.
- Uppercase + wide tracking: "textbook approach to add classiness", but the author is self-conscious using it frequently (p.600) so the technique is double-edged.
- Copywork is pitched as plagiarism-free learning (private practice); it is not advice to ship copies. [engine note] The engine must keep copywork out of client output; use it only for studying references.
- [engine note] The "tan card" regret and "cards should be neat" is the only explicit negative design rule in the chapter; the book gives no definition of neat.
- [engine note] The 60/30/10 figure is a proportion illustration, not tied to a stated rule here. If the engine uses 60-30-10, cite another chapter or mark as engine-added.

- [engine note] (audit 2026-10-15) The p.593 pastel demo puts white text on the lightest pastel tile (sky blue). The "pastel is relaxing" lesson does not excuse contrast: pastel fills usually need dark text, or the white label fails WCAG. The book does not discuss this.
